## Quick answer

- What it is: A 4 min 52 s camera fly-through of one December 2010 'pineapple express' atmospheric river in MERRA-2 reanalysis; model time is NOT linear: it fast-forwards from Oct 1 2010, freezes, and jumps backward at least 6 times to replay Dec 6-14 2010 from new camera angles.
- Kind: data-animation
- Data source: NASA MERRA-2 reanalysis (page credits: 'MERRA-2', 'Winds | platform: MERRA-2 | type: Model', and 'GTOPO30 Topography and Bathymetry'). Description: clouds and precipitation are from MERRA-2, 'a retrospective blend of a weather model and conventional and satellite observations', hourly since 1980. Layers named on the page: clouds, precipitation, water vapor transport (blue 3D shading), winds, snow, snow depth, soil moisture. (model)
- Period covered: VERIFIED from the DATE_ONLY overlay: earliest label 'Oct 01 2010 00:00' (movie t=0.0 s), latest label 'Dec 14 2010 00:00' (t=44.0 s and t=61.0 s). That is 31+30+13 = 74 days of model time touched. The story part is Dec 6-14 2010; the final frame (frame-set index 18000) reads 'Dec 11 2010 00:00'. No time zone is printed on the label (UTC is an inference from MERRA-2 convention, not verified).
- Time step of the underlying data: MERRA-2 is hourly per the page description ('a dataset that provides hourly information around the globe since 1980'). The overlay shows minutes such as 05:42 and 18:43, so the renderer interpolates between hourly fields (inference) in slow sections and skips fields in the opening fast-forward (85 min per frame, verified).
- **One frame equals**: VARIABLE, there is no single value. Verified rates (DATE_ONLY mp4, 59.94 fps, frame = t x 59.94): (a) opening fast-forward: frame 0 'Oct 01 00:00' -> frame 1 'Oct 01 01:25' = 85 min/frame; t=10.0 s (frame 599) 'Nov 05 11:39' = 51099 min / 599 frames = 85.3 min/frame; (b) t=10 -> 20 s: 'Nov 29 18:29', 34970 min / 600 frames = 58.3 min/frame (slowing); (c) t=20 -> 25.02 s: 'Dec 06 05:42', 9313 min / 300.5 frames = 31.0 min/frame; (d) frames 2700 -> 3600 (t=45.05 -> 60.06 s): exactly 9 min/frame: 'Dec 08 09:00' at frame 2700, 'Dec 10 05:51' at frame 2999 (299 x 9 = 2691 min = 44 h 51 min, matches), 'Dec 13 23:24' at frame 3596 (896 x 9 = 8064 min = 5 d 14 h 24 min, matches); (e) 0 min/frame (time frozen) from at least t=75.07 s to t=96.76 s, both 'Dec 11 18:43' (1300 frames, 21.7 s); (f) closing: 'Dec 06 13:10' at t=275.27 s -> 'Dec 11 00:00' at last frame = 6410 min / 1000.5 frames = 6.4 min/frame.

NASA caption: surface layers are stacked in that order (snow on top, ocean at the bottom) and a layer turns transparent where its value is very low. From the page description: translucent blue 3D shading = water vapor transport (the atmospheric river); white = clouds. White streaks = winds (credited 'Winds, MERRA-2'); the teal/green/purple ocean tints have no legend on the page (unknown).
- Fit as a sonification source: **fair** — The picture is a moving-camera 3D scene whose model time freezes and jumps backward at least 6 times, so pixel position does not map to a place and frame number does not map to a date; a naive 'scan the frame, one frame = one step in time' sonifier would produce nonsense. It is still usable because NASA supplies a frame-accurate date overlay (rare in this gallery), the layers use strongly distinct colors (saturated blue plume, yellow precipitation), there is one clean stretch with a verified constant 9 min per frame (t=45.05-60.06 s, Dec 8 09:00 to Dec 14 00:00), and the narrated version plus VTT captions is ready-made accessible audio description.
- Suggested mapping: Use the silent HD movie from t=45.05 s to 60.06 s (frames 2700-3600, 9 model minutes per frame). Per frame: count saturated-blue pixels (water vapor transport plume) -> pitch and loudness of a sustained drone; count yellow/orange pixels (liquid precipitation, top of the 0-0.001 kg/m^2/s scale) -> density of short rain-like ticks; pan left/right by the plume centroid x. Speak the model date from a pre-built DATE_ONLY lookup every 6 model hours (every 40 frames). Treat the numbers as 'how much of the screen is plume', not as physical units, because the camera changes the pixel area.

## Gotchas

- Model time is non-monotonic. The same days (Dec 11-14 2010) are replayed several times; frame order is story order, not time order.
- Time is frozen at 'Dec 11 2010 18:43' for at least 21.7 s (t=75.07 to 96.76 s) while the camera keeps moving, and holds at 'Dec 14 2010 00:00' are seen at t=44.0 and t=61.0 s. Motion on screen during those stretches is camera only.
- Dates in file and folder names (2021-11-29_1351, 2021-12-16_1355, 2021-12-10_1233) are render tags, not data dates. dated_sets.json decodes the 7680x2160 PNG set as first/last 2021-12-16 with step 0 s: ignore that entry for timing.
- atmos_river_DATE_ONLY_HD.webm is 30 fps / 583.37 s while the .mp4 is 59.94 fps / 291.99 s for the same ~17501 frames: the webm runs at half speed. Earlier samples taken from the webm near t=89.95 s ('Dec 10 23:53', 'Dec 11 04:30') do not agree with the EXR frame at the same index (index 03200 = 'Dec 08 09:00'); trust the mp4 and EXR.
- Frame counts differ: silent movie ~17486 frames / 17485 EXR files (16 indices missing between 00500 and 18000: 13 gaps of one frame and one gap of three), DATE_ONLY 17501 frames. Matching by frame number drifts by up to 16 frames (0.27 s); matching by time is close enough for sound.
- The narrated cut is a different length (287.20 s vs 291.72 s) with a black opening, a title card and a NASA logo ending, so silent-movie timestamps and the DATE_ONLY lookup cannot be assumed to line up with it.
- Cross-origin pixel reading is blocked: a HEAD request with 'Origin: http://localhost:3000' returned 'access-control-allow-origin: https://tempo.multiverse.music', so a canvas that draws this video straight from svs.gsfc.nasa.gov will be tainted (getImageData throws). Download the files and serve them from the team's own origin, or pre-compute features offline.
- All movies are 59.94 fps; 1080p60 decoding plus per-frame canvas analysis is heavy in a browser. Sample every Nth frame or pre-compute.
- The only browser-friendly frame set is 7680x2160 PNG at 16.6 MB per frame (1798 files, 32.1 GB, indices 04200-07800 step 2, about t=61.7-121.8 s of the silent movie, which includes the frozen-time stretch). The other sets are EXR (427.5 GB and 573.3 GB), not decodable by browsers.
- The date label has no time zone and no seconds; UTC is an assumption.
- Text in the page is slightly stale or inconsistent: 'Coming soon to our YouTube channel' (January 2022 wording); the 7680x2160 PNG frame set is listed under both the narrated and the silent media group; the narration lists 'New Zealand, Greenland, Antarctica, and the Middle East' while the page text also lists Europe.

##### Media group 315481 | widget: Basic text with HTML | title: (none)
Description: Features in Earth’s atmosphere, spawned by the heat of the Sun and the rotation of the Earth, transport water and energy around the globe. Clouds and precipitation shown here are from NASA’s MERRA-2 reanalysis, a retrospective blend of a weather model and conventional and satellite observations.
Within the mid-latitudes, winds move clouds from west to east. Within the tropics easterly trade winds converge along the equator to create a moisture rich cluster of clouds, convection, and precipitation called the intertropical convergence zone, or ITCZ. Disturbances in its flow transport immense amounts of moisture and energy from the tropics to the poles. Studies have shown that atmospheric rivers account for the vast majority of the poleward transport of water vapor.
The American Meteorological Society defines an atmospheric river as “a long, narrow, and transient corridor of strong horizontal water vapor transport that is typically associated with a low-level jet stream ahead of the cold front of an extratropical cyclone.” A common measure for the strength of an atmospheric river is the integrated water vapor transport, or the amount of moisture that is moved from one place to another by the flow of the atmosphere. The blue shading shown here gives a three-dimensional view of the water vapor transport. Tropical moisture is pulled in from the ITCZ and in this example, converges with other moisture sources to form an atmospheric river. The feature then travels towards the west coast of the United States as a sub-class of atmospheric rivers commonly referred to as the “pineapple express” due to its origin near Hawai’i.
The atmospheric river is guided by the semi-permanent sub-tropical high pressure off the coast of California and the Baja Peninsula as well as the Aleutian low in the Gulf of Alaska. The pressure gradient between the clockwise flow of the Californian high and the counterclockwise flow of the Aleutian low funnel the atmospheric moisture into a narrow corridor. The more intense the pressure gradient is, the stronger the winds are that transport the water vapor. Extreme rainfall has also been associated with the more intense gradients.
Much of the moisture stays close to the surface but the rising motion of the low pressure to the north results in the air cooling, condensing the water vapor into a liquid. Precipitation over the ocean falls along the feature’s cold front on its northern side.
Another way that air can rise and condense into precipitation is through orographic lift. When air encounters the mountains along the west coast of the United States, it is forced upwards. The rising air becomes saturated, causing rain and snow to fall, particularly on the windward side of the mountain. The flow of air continues eastward, depleted of its moisture.
The precipitation that falls because of atmospheric rivers is important for the hydrologic cycle in the western United States. The winter buildup of the snowpack provides valuable freshwater resources. Despite being beneficial at times, atmospheric river induced precipitation can also be destructive. The occurrence of extreme atmospheric river precipitation events, such as the one that occurred in this example, can result in widespread flooding and mudslides.
Atmospheric rivers are not unique to the west coast of North America and occur around the globe, including Europe, New Zealand, the Middle East, Greenland, and Antarctica. The study of global phenomenon such as atmospheric rivers over the past four decades is made possible through NASA’s MERRA-2 reanalysis, a spatially and temporally consistent blend of satellite and conventional observations with a numerical model. With a dataset that provides hourly information around the globe since 1980, there is still much that can be learned about Earth’s atmosphere and the transport of water and energy around the globe.
