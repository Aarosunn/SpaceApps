## Quick answer

- What it is: Daily-step map of IMERG precipitation anomaly (departure from the long-term average, each day's anomaly added and faded over about three months); one frame = one calendar day from 2024-07-01 to 2026-11-12, re-rendered once a day.
- Kind: data-animation
- Data source: IMERG precipitation compared with an IMERG climatology (record starts June 2000 per the page text). VERIFIED from bundle text: 'each day's variation from the average precipitation is added to the map, then allowed to gradually fade away over three months'. (observation)
- Period covered: 2024-07-01 to 2026-11-12 (calendar span 865 days; 863 frames because 2024-11-26 and 2024-11-27 are missing). Older frames 2020-01-01 to 2024-06-30 (1637 files) sit in not_published/ sub-folders. INFERRED: the published window has a fixed start (2024-07-01) and grows by one frame per day rather than rolling.
- Time step of the underlying data: 1 day per frame (file names step 86400 s). The underlying IMERG data is half-hourly, but each frame is a daily update of a decaying ~3-month accumulation of anomalies, shown in mm per day.
- **One frame equals**: 1 calendar day. Arithmetic: movie 57.53 s x 15 fps = 863 frames = 863 files in the frame sets; first label '01 Jul 2024', last label '12 Nov 2026' = 864 days apart, minus the 2 missing days = 862 steps for 863 frames = 1 day per frame. Cross-check: frame 285 label '14 Apr 2025' = 2024-07-01 + 287 days (285 + 2 skipped); frame 570 label '24 Jan 2026' = 2024-07-01 + 572 days. At 15 fps, 1 s of movie = 15 days, one year = about 24.3 s.
- **One frame = one day?**: yes
- Where a program gets a frame's date: Best: file name (YYYY.MM.DD) in flatcomposite, flatalpha, flatcompwithdates, flatdates, Robinson and RobinsonWithDates sets. Also burned-in labels (OCR) and the separate flatdates set (small transparent PNG with only the white date text 'YYYY-MM-DD', verified on imergvar_dates.now.png = '2026-11-12'). The numberedcomposite set has index-only names (00000 to 00592) and is stale: avoid it.
- Date label seen on screen: Both movies: white text 'DD Mon YYYY' (e.g. '12 Nov 2026') in a dark rounded box at the bottom-left corner. The 1920x1080 flatcomposite frames / imergvar_1080p.now.png use a different layout: 'YYYY-MM-DD' (e.g. '2026-11-12') at top-left of centre, next to the title 'IMERG Seasonal Precipitation Variations' and the colorbar. No time zone shown (daily data).
- Update behaviour: Re-rendered once a day around 06:00 UTC (VERIFIED for today only: newest frame files and now.png Last-Modified 2026-11-14 06:00:16-17 UTC, movies 06:04:15 and 06:05:54 UTC; the stale numberedcomposite frame 00592 is also stamped 06:00:14 UTC on 2026-02-18, which supports a fixed 06:00 UTC job). Newest data date 2026-11-12, so the lag is about 1.25 days from the end of that day (2 calendar days). Only the new day's frame is added; old frame files are not rewritten (imergvar_dates.2024.07.01.png Last-Modified 2025-03-31). Movies and *.now.png are overwritten in place. Window is not rolling: currently 865 days and growing (inferred).
- Status on 2026-11-14: updating-daily — newest data: 2026-11-12
- View / camera: Movie 1 and flat frame sets: flat equirectangular world map, fixed camera, Pacific-centred (Africa split across the left and right edges). Central meridian is NOT 0 deg: estimated from landmarks at about 160 deg W (left edge about 20 deg E); not documented on the page. Movie 2 and the Robinson sets: same data in Robinson projection (oval map), same centring. 3600x1800 sets are 2:1; 1080p is 16:9.
- On-screen clutter: Movies: only the date box bottom-left; no colorbar, no title, no logo. Land is flat grey shaded relief, ocean black. 1080p flatcomposite frames and imergvar_1080p.now.png: title 'IMERG Seasonal Precipitation Variations', date, and a colorbar (-20 -15 -10 -5 -2 2 5 10 15 20, 'mm per day') across the top, overlapping the Arctic part of the map.
- Clean variant: Yes. flatalpha (3600x1800 RGBA PNG): anomaly colours only, transparent elsewhere (VERIFIED on imergvar_alpha.now.png: colour type 6, first pixel [0,0,0,0], no text). flatdates: date text only. 3600x1800 flatcomposite and Robinson: INFERRED to be data over grey land without date (the 'withdates' twins flatcompwithdates / RobinsonWithDates carry the date); not opened.
- What the colours mean: Diverging scale in mm per day, -20 to +20, read from the colorbar in imergvar_1080p.now.png: browns/tans = drier than the seasonal average (dark brown about -2 to -5, pale tan about -20), black = near normal (-2 to +2), teal = wetter than average (dark teal about +2 to +5, pale mint/white about +20). The colour steps are discrete bands (10 bins), and values are the faded ~3-month accumulation, not a single day's rain.
- Fit as a sonification source: **good** — True one-frame-per-day cadence with the date in the file name, a clean transparent data layer, a simple two-sided colour scale (wetter vs drier) that maps naturally onto two contrasting timbres, and slow large-scale patterns (for example the equatorial Pacific band) that stay audible over time. Held back from excellent because each frame is a smoothed ~3-month accumulation in 10 discrete colour bands, the meaning ('difference from normal') needs explaining to listeners, and the map centring is undocumented.
- Suggested mapping: Use flatalpha frames (weekly or daily). Per frame and per region (for example 6 longitude sectors of the tropics), measure teal area and brightness vs brown area and brightness: teal drives a bright, rising tone (wetter than normal), brown a dry, low woodblock or filtered-noise tone (drier than normal), brightness of the colour sets loudness; near-black is silence. One frame per beat at 15 frames per second gives one year in about 24 s; announce the month from the file name.

## Gotchas

- One frame = one day, but the picture is NOT that day's rainfall: it is a running anomaly (departure from climatology) with each day's contribution fading over about three months.
- Two days are missing (2024-11-26, 2024-11-27): the page says 865 items, the folders hold 863 and the movie has 863 frames, so 'start date + index' is off by 2 days after index 147.
- The movie is not an encode of the 1920x1080 flatcomposite frames: the movie has a 'DD Mon YYYY' box bottom-left and no colorbar; the 1080p frames have a title, 'YYYY-MM-DD' date and colorbar at the top.
- numberedcomposite (593 files, 00000-00592; page says 595) is stale: last file Last-Modified 2026-02-18, first 2025-04-15. Its index -> date mapping is unknown. Do not use.
- imergvar_1080p.now_print.jpg is stale (Last-Modified 2026-01-27, shows 2026-01-24) while imergvar_1080p.now.png is current (2026-11-12).
- Pacific-centred map with central meridian near 160 deg W (estimate): longitude maths from page 4285 (centred on 0 deg) does not carry over.
- File naming is inconsistent across sets: imergvar_composite.*, imergvar_alpha.*, imergvar_dates.*, imerganom_w_date.* (with-dates sets), and '.rob.png' suffix for Robinson.
- Movie length grows by 1/15 s per day; frame counts in this record are a snapshot of 2026-11-14.
- CORS: NASA's server sends 'access-control-allow-origin: https://tempo.multiverse.music' (checked on page 4285 files on the same host), so browser canvas pixel reads need the files served from your own origin.
- flatdates strip size varies (bundle: 195x63 for the set, 199x63 for now.png; downloaded now.png decodes as 199x51); white text on transparent.

Description: The Global Precipitation Meaurement (GPM) mission produces NASA's most comprehensive estimate of global rain and snowfall, the Integrated Multi-satellite Retrievals for GPM, or IMERG.  IMERG is largely based on data from the GPM constellation - a group of satellites independently operated by many agencies that pool their data for global precipitation estimates.  These satellites currently include the GPM Core Observatory, other U.S. civilian satellites (Aqua, NOAA-19, NOAA-20, Suomi NPP), U.S. defense satellites (DMSP F-16, DMSP F-17, DMSP- F18), and international agency satellites (GCOM-W1, Metop-B, Metop-C, and MeghaTropiques).  The IMERG dataset starts in June 2000, providing a sufficiently long record that seasonal/regional averages, or climatology, can be computed with reasonable confidence.  By comparing the progression of the observed precipitation with climatology, we can generate maps of showing how much observations depart from normal.  In the animation, each day's variation from the average precipitation is added to the map, then allowed to gradually fade away over three months so that time changes in the larger-scale patterns are easier to see.  Researchers sometimes refer to these deviations from the climatology as "precipitation anomalies".
