- Kind: data-animation
- Data source: DYAMOND run of NASA's coupled GEOS-MITgcm model on the NASA Advanced Supercomputing facility (bundle lines 15-16: dataset 'DYAMOND', type 'Model'). Variable: atmospheric CO2 concentration in ppm, volume-rendered over a textured Earth. It is a simulation, not a direct measurement; the page text says GEOS ingests ground and satellite observations (MODIS, VIIRS). No mission is credited. (model)
- Period covered: VERIFIED from the burned-in labels of the orbit movie: first frame 14 Jan 2020 22:00, last frame 03 Mar 2020 21:00. - Fit as a sonification source: **good** — Strong points: an exact clock (1 frame = 1 hour, date computable from the frame index), a built-in rhythm (the day / night 'pulsing' NASA describes repeats every 24 frames), a simple colour code on a black background (warm = high CO2), almost no on-screen text, and a legible 7 MB movie. What keeps it from excellent: every version has a moving camera and there is no flat map, so a fixed pixel never means a fixed place; colours come from volume rendering over a textured base map, so they are not exact ppm; and it covers only 49 days of one winter, so there is no long-term trend to tell.
- Suggested mapping: Use the orbit movie. For each hourly frame k, inside the fixed globe disc, measure the share of warm pixels (red minus blue above a threshold; ignore white snow) in the left, centre and right thirds. Total share sets the loudness and filter brightness of a sustained pad; left / right balance sets stereo pan; a new bright red blob triggers a short percussive ping. Clock the music to the data: 24 frames = one bar, so each model day is one bar and the day / night CO2 'breathing' becomes the groove (native 30 fps gives 0.8 s per day, 75 cycles per minute; 6 frames per second gives a 4-second bar). Pick the bass note from the region under the camera (centre longitude = 140.0 - 0.2017 x k degrees east) and announce region and date (2020-01-14 22:00 + k hours) as text for screen readers. Cheapest build: compute this 1176-row table once offline and let the browser play the video and read the table by frame index.

## Frame → time mapping

k = 0-based frame index. A frame's timestamp in the movie is k / 30 s, so k = floor(currentTime x 30 + 0.001), or round(mediaTime x 30) from requestVideoFrameCallback. Frame-set file number n = k + 1 (00001 to 01176). time(k) = 2020-01-14 22:00 + k hours. Same thing split up: calendar day = 14 Jan + floor((k + 22) / 24), hour of day = (k + 22) mod 24; a new day starts when k mod 24 = 2 (k = 2 is 15 Jan 00:00). Arithmetic: label at t = 0 s is 14 Jan 22:00, label at t = 10 s is 27 Jan 10:00, difference 12 d 12 h = 300 h over 10 s x 30 fps = 300 frames, so 1.000 h per frame; whole clip 14 Jan 22:00 to 03 Mar 21:00 = 1175 h over 1175 frame steps, so 1.000 h per frame. The formula reproduced all 12 labels read (k = 0, 9, 199, 300, 570, 600, 601, 602, 870, 1140, 1161, 1175). VERIFIED only for the orbit version. For the four label-free versions it is INFERRED: same 1176 frames, and their CO2 pattern over North America at k = 1161 matches the orbit frame labelled 03 Mar 07:00. Time zone is not printed; UTC is inferred.

## Gotchas

- Not one image per day: one frame is one model HOUR. 24 frames make a day; the whole 39.2 s clip is 49 days.
- NASA's description says 'January-March of 2020' but the frames run only from 14 Jan 2020 22:00 to 03 Mar 2020 21:00 (first and last frame labels).
- NASA's text calls it a 'global map', but no flat map is among the files: all 8 movies are globe views with a camera that keeps moving through the clip (orbit, three push-in zooms, one fly-through; 6 movies sampled, the other 2 are higher-resolution encodes of the same moves by file name). A fixed pixel is not a fixed place.
- Only the orbit version has a date on screen. The eastern zoom, 5x3 zoom, western zoom and tilt have none; use time = 2020-01-14 22:00 + k hours (inferred for these four).
- Dates inside file names (12-1-2023b, 3-29-2023a, 4-3-2023a, 1-22-2025a, 12-4-2023b) are production stamps, not data dates; one is even a year off (the '3-29-2023a' frames carry render stamp 2024:03:29 in their EXR header).
- The date label has no time zone. UTC is assumed (model time); the night-time CO2 build-up seen over South America at 07:00 and its absence at 21:00 fits UTC.
- The smallest movie (7.2 MB) is HEVC with the 'hev1' tag: browsers decode HEVC only on some systems. For a browser build transcode it once to H.264 or use the 71.8 MB 2160p H.264 file.
- Browser pixel reads straight from NASA will fail: the server answers 'access-control-allow-origin: https://tempo.multiverse.music' whatever Origin is sent (checked on both orbit MP4s, one request sent with a localhost Origin), so a canvas that draws these videos or images from svs.gsfc.nasa.gov becomes tainted and getImageData throws. Download the file and serve it from the same origin as the app, on http://localhost rather than file:// (standard browser behaviour, not tested here). Plain playback in a video tag is not affected.
- Frame sets are not practical: five are OpenEXR (not browser-decodable, from about 22 MB to well over 60 MB per frame, 28.6 to 72.4 GB per set) and the only PNG set is the 5x3 version at 9600x3240, about 17.5 MB per frame on average, 20.6 GB in total. Pull frames from an MP4 instead.
- Pixel colour is not ppm: it is a volume rendering blended over a base map. Brightness is misleading (snow and ice are white, the Bahamas shallows are turquoise, the orbit globe darkens toward the right limb). Use a warm-hue index such as red minus blue, and crop to the globe disc.
- In the orbit version a whole-disc average mixes real change with which continent is in view, and local time of day dominates: South America is saturated red-orange at 03 Mar 07:00 and almost colourless at 03 Mar 21:00, 14 frames later.
- The tilt version is unusable for pixel reading in its middle part: frames at 9.9 s and 19.5 s are dark and blurred (NASA says so too).
- Page metadata is unreliable: four frame sets are listed with '0 Item(s)' and one with 1177, but every folder holds 1176 files; the zoom .hwshow file is labelled 230 bytes but is 642 bytes; sizes on the page are in MiB (6.9 MB on the page = 7,228,199 bytes).
- The 5x3 frame folders are named 9600x3240_16x9_30p although 9600x3240 is 80:27, not 16:9.
- No movie has an audio track, and the 2160p H.264 runs at 14.6 Mb/s: downscale before sampling.
- Each EXR frame header (first 1 KB) stores the camera matrix, so an exact camera pose can be read with a 4 KB range request per frame; sample a few frames and interpolate rather than looping over all 1176.

Description: What we’re looking at

This global map shows concentrations of carbon dioxide as the gas moved through Earth’s atmosphere from January through March 2020, driven by wind patterns and atmospheric circulation. 

Because of the model’s high resolution, you can zoom in and see carbon dioxide emissions rising from power plants, fires, and cities, then spreading across continents and oceans.  

“As policymakers and as scientists, we're trying to account for where carbon comes from and how that impacts the planet,” said NASA climate scientist Lesley Ott. “You see here how everything is interconnected by these different weather patterns.”

##### Media group 375031 | widget: Basic text with HTML | title: (none)
Description: What are the sources of CO2? 

Over China, the United States, and South Asia, the majority of emissions came from power plants, industrial facilities, and cars and trucks, Ott said. Meanwhile, in Africa and South America, emissions largely stemmed from fires, especially those related to land management, controlled agricultural burns and deforestation, along with the burning of oil and coal. Fires release carbon dioxide as they burn. 

Why does the map look like it’s pulsing? 

There are two primary reasons for the pulsing: First, fires have a clear day-night cycle. They typically flare up during the day and die down at night. 

Second, you’re seeing the absorption and release of carbon dioxide as trees and plants photosynthesize. Earth’s land and oceans absorb about 50% of carbon dioxide; these are natural carbon sinks. Plants take up carbon dioxide during the day as they photosynthesize and then release it at night through respiration. Notice that much of the pulsing occurred in regions with lots of trees, like mid- or high-latitude forests. And since the data were taken during the Southern Hemisphere summer, you see more pulsing in the tropics and South America, where it was the active growing season. 

Some of the pulsing also comes from the planetary boundary layer — the lowest 3,000 feet 914 meters of the atmosphere — which rises as the Earth’s surface is heated by sunlight during the day, then falls as it cools at night.

Description: The data that drives it  

The map was created by NASA’s Scientific Visualization Studio using a model called GEOS, short for the Goddard Earth Observing System. GEOS is a high-resolution weather reanalysis model, powered by supercomputers, that is used to represent what was happening in the atmosphere — including storm systems, cloud formations, and other natural events. GEOS pulls in billions of data points from ground observations and satellite instruments, such as the Terra satellite’s MODIS and the Suomi-NPP satellite’s VIIRS instruments. Its resolution is more than 100 times greater than your typical weather model. 

Ott and other climate scientists wanted to know what GEOS would show if it was used to model the movement and density of carbon dioxide in the global atmosphere. 

“We had this opportunity to say: can we tag along and see what really high-resolution CO2 looks like?” Ott said. “We had a feeling we were going to see plume structures and things that we've never been able to see when we do these coarser resolution simulations.” 

Her instinct was right. “Just seeing how persistent the plumes were and the interaction of the plumes with weather systems, it was tremendous.” 

Why it matters

You can’t tackle climate change without confronting the fact that we’re emitting massive amounts of CO2, and it’s warming the atmosphere, Ott said. 

Carbon dioxide is a heat-trapping greenhouse gas and the primary reason for Earth’s rising temperatures. As CO2 builds in the atmosphere, it warms our planet. This is clear in the numbers. 2023 was the hottest year on record, according to scientists from NASA’s Goddard Institute for Space Studies (GISS) in New York. Most of the ten hottest years on record have occurred in the past decade.

All this carbon dioxide isn’t harmful to air quality. In fact, we need some carbon dioxide to keep the planet warm enough for life to exist. But when too much CO2 is pumped into the atmosphere, the Earth warms too much and too fast. That’s what has been happening for at least the past half century. The concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere increased from approximately 278 parts per million in 1750, the beginning of the industrial era, to 427 parts per million in May 2024. More on that here.

Human activities have “unequivocally caused warming,” according to the latest report by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change. This warming is leading to all sorts of changes to our climate, including more intense storms, wildfires, heat waves, and rising sea levels.

Description: Inside the SVS studio

Carbon dioxide exists everywhere in the atmosphere, and the challenge for AJ Christensen, a senior visualization designer at NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center, was to show the differences in density of this invisible gas.

"We didn't want people to get the impression that there was no carbon dioxide in these sparser regions," Christensen said. “But we also wanted to really highlight the dense regions because that's the interesting feature of the data. We were trying to show that there's a lot of density over New York and Beijing.”  

Data visualizations help people understand how Earth’s systems work, and they can help scientists find patterns in massive datasets, Ott said. 

“What's happening is you're stitching together this very complex array of models to make use of the different satellite data, and that’s helping us fill in this broad puzzle of all the processes that control carbon dioxide,” Ott said. “The hope is that if we understand greenhouse gases really well today, we'll be able to build models that better predict them over the next decades or even centuries.”
