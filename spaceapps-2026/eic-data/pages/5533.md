## Quick answer

- What it is: GEOS-CF model animation of four surface pollutants (CO, PM2.5, NOx, O3) for 1 Jul to 14 Sep 2024, shown as scrolling vertical colour strips over one world map (or one rotating globe); 1 frame = 1 model hour in the 16:9 map, no dates and no colorbar on screen.
- Kind: data-animation
- Data source: NASA GMAO GEOS-CF (Goddard Earth Observing System Composition Forecasting) model output; bundles/5533.md line 15 credits 'GEOS-CF ... date_range: July 1-September 14, 2024', https://portal.nccs.nasa.gov/datashare/gmao/geos-cf/v2/ (model)
- Period covered: INFERRED: 2024-07-01 00:00 to 2024-09-14 00:00 (75 days = 1800 hours). The 1800-frame map set holds hours 0..1799, i.e. 2024-07-01T00 through 2024-09-13T23. Basis: (1) bundle line 15 dataset field 'July 1-September 14, 2024' (NASA metadata, the ONLY source of the start date); (2) VERIFIED by eye by the verifier that the 16:9 map movie repeats its day/night pattern every 24 frames (frames 870, 874, 882, 894), and 1800 frames / 24 = 75.0 days; Jul 1 + 75 d = Sep 14. NASA's captions say only 'July-August 2024' (disagreement, see gotchas). No date is printed on any frame.
- Time step of the underlying data: 1 hour per frame in the 16:9 map (verified indirectly: diurnal ozone/NOx pattern has a 24-frame period; +4 frames looks the same, +12 frames is the opposite phase, +24 frames matches again). Whether the fields are hourly instantaneous or hourly means is unknown.

(a) AirQuality_MapWithLabels 16:9 movie and PNG set (1800 frames): 1 frame = 1 hour, 24 frames = 1 day, 1 s of 30 fps video = 30 hours (VERIFIED by diurnal period). (b) AirQuality_SphereWithLabels (1801 frames): 1 hour, INFERRED from the frame count only (1801 = 1800 intervals + 1). (c) Sphere long (3031 frames): INFERRED about 0.594 hour (35.6 min) = 1800 h / 3030 intervals; not verified, low confidence. (d) EIC 32:9 PNG frame set and EIC_AirQuality_NMNH_7680x2160.mp4 (4502 frames each): about 0.4 hour (24 min) per frame = 1800 h / 4501 intervals, i.e. 60 frames = 1 day = 1 s at 60 fps. Full-period coverage is VERIFIED at the end point for both (PNG index 4501 and NMNH t=74.9 s both show the same picture, which matches the map's last frame); the 0.4 h figure is arithmetic, and whether intermediate frames are time-interpolated or held is unknown. - On-screen clutter: Low but structural. Per strip one semi-transparent dark pill label near the bottom (flat map, y about 77% of height) or on the equator (globe); labels move with the strips. Coastlines and borders drawn in black. No colorbar, legend, date, title or logo. The strip boundaries themselves are hard vertical edges that move every frame.
- Clean variant: No. Every movie and frame set on this page is the 'WithLabels' composite; no label-free, single-pollutant, alpha or date-only layer. Single-pollutant versions live on related pages 5151 (PM2.5), 5152 (O3), 5153 (CO), 5154 (NOx) (not analysed here).
- What the colours mean: Each pollutant has its own colour ramp; darker / more saturated = higher concentration (INFERRED, no colorbar or units anywhere). CO: pale yellow -> orange -> red -> dark red. PM2.5: cream -> orange -> brown. NOx: light green -> dark green, with black/white patches over the Southern Ocean and Antarctica (apparently the bottom of the scale). O3: black/grey -> lavender -> deep violet-blue, with pale grey/white patches over land at night. Numeric values cannot be recovered from pixels.
- Fit as a sonification source: **fair** — Strong content (hourly model data, obvious day/night pulse in O3 and NOx every 24 frames, dramatic wildfire CO/PM2.5 plumes, four pollutants that map naturally to four voices), but hard for a pixel reader: no date on screen, no colorbar so no physical values, and a given pixel switches pollutant every 180 frames (6 s) because the strips scroll, so the program must track strip position by formula before any pixel means anything. Globe versions add rotation and are worse. Also not one frame per day.
- Suggested mapping: Use the 16:9 flat map. For frame i and pixel column x (at 1280-px-wide scale) the pollutant is idx = floor((x + i) / 180) mod 4 -> 0 CO, 1 PM2.5, 2 NOx, 3 O3 (at 5760 px: floor((x + 4.5*i) / 810) mod 4); the verifier checked this formula against frames 0, 870, 882, 894 and 1799. Give each pollutant its own instrument (CO low drone, PM2.5 filtered noise, NOx pulse, O3 high pad); for each visible strip take mean colour darkness/saturation as loudness and brightness, strip x-position as stereo pan, and announce the region under the strip by speech. Play 24 frames per musical bar so one bar = one day and the diurnal ozone cycle becomes rhythm; speak the computed date (Jul 1 2024 + i hours) at each bar.

## Frame → time mapping

16:9 flat map (recommended): datetime(i) = 2024-07-01T00:00Z + i * 1 hour, i = 0..1799 (file AirQuality_WithLabels_5760x3240_{i:04d}.png; movie time t = i/30 s). Last frame i=1799 -> 1799 h = 74 d 23 h -> 2024-09-13T23:00Z. Day number = i // 24. Arithmetic: 1800 frames / 24 frames per day = 75 days; Jul 1 + 31 (Aug 1) + 31 (Sep 1) + 13 = Sep 14. The hour of frame 0 is ASSUMED 00:00 UTC (not printed anywhere); a day/night phase check supports it to within roughly +/-3 hours: frame 870 (870 mod 24 = 6 -> 06Z) has dark night-time NOx over North America and strong afternoon ozone over East China; frame 882 (18Z) has strong ozone over South America and pale NOx over North America. Sphere short: same formula, i = 0..1800 (inferred). EIC PNG frame set and NMNH movie: hours since start = i * 1800/4501 (about 0.4 h), i = 0..4501; end point verified, linearity in between inferred. EIC Preview movie: hours since start = about i * 0.2, i = 0..4501, ends near hour 900 = 2024-08-07T12Z (inferred from Preview t = NMNH t/2). ## Gotchas

- NOT one image per day: 1 frame = 1 model hour in the 16:9 map (24 frames per day); other files use other, non-integer steps.
- No date anywhere on screen and none in file names: dates exist only via index formula, and the start (Jul 1 2024, assumed 00:00 UTC) comes from NASA metadata, not from the files.
- Description vs evidence: all four captions (bundle lines 39, 58, 72, 87) say 'during July-August 2024', the dataset field says 'July 1-September 14, 2024'. Measured cadence (24 frames/day x 1800 frames = 75 days) agrees with the dataset field, so the captions understate the span by about two weeks.
- The four pollutants are not four panels of the same map. They are scrolling strips over one map: at any instant each region shows only one pollutant. Strips move left 1 px per frame at 1280 scale (4.5 px at 5760), strip width 180 px (810 px), i.e. 10 strip widths over the 1800 frames; a fixed pixel changes pollutant every 180 frames in the order CO -> PM2.5 -> NOx -> O3.
- No colorbar or units: colour cannot be converted to concentration; only relative intensity within one pollutant's ramp.
- On-screen label reads 'Nitrous Oxides (NOx)' while the page text says 'Nitrogen Oxides'; NOx means nitrogen oxides (NO + NO2). Do not copy the label into narration.
- The two EIC movies have the same length and frame count but different content timing: EIC_AirQuality_NMNH_7680x2160.mp4 at 18.705 s and 37.5 s shows the same picture as EIC_AirQuality_Preview_3840x1080.mp4 at 37.41 s and 74.53 s. The Preview stops about halfway through the data (about 7 Aug 2024). The 4502-frame PNG set follows the NMNH movie, not the Preview.
- EIC frame folder and file names say '30p' / '30fps' but both EIC movies are 60 fps (4502 frames / 60 = 75.03 s; it would be 150.07 s at 30 fps). The Preview's half speed is what a 30 fps playback cut off at 75 s would look like (inference).
- NMNH movie is HEVC 7680x2160, 562.8 MB: not reliably browser-playable. 5760x3240 H.264 is also too large for many decoders.
- Globe versions rotate (about 3.5 deg/s) and the lune strips also slide, so pixel -> place and pixel -> pollutant both change every frame.
- The 16:9 flat map does not appear to show the whole world: at about 4 px per degree only about 320 degrees of longitude fit, and a slice of the central Pacific seems to be cut (by eye; measure before building a pixel -> longitude table).
- Long globe file naming is inconsistent: folder AirQuality_SphereWithLabels_long, files AirQuality_SphereLong_..., movie AirQuality_Sphere_5760x3240_long.mp4. The long version is not the short one plus extra footage: at t=60.0 s both show the same camera view but different data.
- Frame sets are 14 to 37 GB each; never bulk-download.

#### Page description (verbatim, HTML stripped)
NASA utilizes satellite instruments and models to monitor sources of air pollutants and their movement through the atmosphere. This visualization shows concentrations of air pollutants, such as Particulate Matter (PM2.5, fine particles smaller than 2.5 micrometers), Ozone (O~3~), Carbon Monoxide (CO), and Nitrogen Oxides (NO~x~) as they are tracked from NASA's Goddard Earth Observing System Composition Forecasting (GEOS-CF) system.

Description: NASA utilizes satellite instruments and models to monitor sources of air pollutants and their movement through the atmosphere. This visualization shows concentrations of air pollutants, such as Particulate Matter (PM2.5, fine particles smaller than 2.5 micrometers), Ozone (O~3~), Carbon Monoxide (CO), and Nitrogen Oxides (NO~x~) as they are tracked from NASA's Goddard Earth Observing System Composition Forecasting (GEOS-CF) system. This data is used around the world to develop new methods for improving local air quality predictions, to understand the impact of pollution on human health, and to improve the quality of NASA satellite datasets.

Predictions of air pollution are created using complex models that combine information about weather and the emissions, transformation, and transport of chemical species and particles. The Goddard Earth Observing System Composition Forecasting (GEOS-CF) system is a research model maintained by NASA’s Global Modeling and Assimilation Office to help scientists understand the causes and impact of air pollution. It is one of the highest resolution and most detailed models of its kind in the world, made possible through ongoing collaborations between NASA and university scientists. GEOS-CF tracks the concentrations of hundreds of gas phase chemical species and dozens of types of particles characterized by their composition and size. It is used by a wide variety of stakeholders around the world to develop new methods for improving local predictions, understanding the impact of pollution on human health, and improving the quality of NASA satellite datasets. Images for this visualization were created by NASA’s Global Modeling and Assimilation Office (GMAO) and produced by NASA's Scientific Visualization Studio (SVS).
