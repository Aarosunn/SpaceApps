## Quick answer

- What it is: Full-disk ultraviolet movie of the Sun from SDO/AIA (gold 171-angstrom look): a rolling 7-day movie and a rolling 24-hour movie, one frame every 108 seconds, re-rendered daily with a UTC timestamp burned in. It is the Sun, not Earth.
- Kind: data-animation
- Data source: NASA Solar Dynamics Observatory (SDO), Atmospheric Imaging Assembly (AIA). Wavelength: 171 angstrom per the page body text and the thumbnail file name (..._SDO_AIA_AIA_171_thumb.jpg); the page's top description says 'AIA-304' (disagreement). 'Color table and image processing applied' (page text), so brightness is not calibrated. (observation)
- Period covered: VERIFIED from frames of the files rendered Nov 14 2026. Week movie: Nov 07, 2026 00:00:08 UTC to Nov 13, 2026 23:58:08 UTC (7 UTC calendar days minus 2 min). 24-hour movie: Nov 12, 2026 23:59:56 UTC to Nov 13, 2026 23:58:08 UTC. Dated archive copies exist back to 2025-09-19 (week) and 2025-09-29 (24 h), with gaps.
- Time step of the underlying data: 108 seconds between consecutive movie frames (computed below). INFERENCE: this is every 9th AIA image, since AIA's native cadence is 12 s (not stated in the evidence; the on-screen seconds :08, :44, :56 fit a 12 s grid).
- **One frame equals**: 108 seconds (1.8 minutes). Arithmetic, 24-hour movie: frame 250 = Nov 13 07:31:44, frame 798 = Nov 13 23:58:08; difference 59184 s over 548 frames = 108.0 s exactly. Week movie: frame 3500 = Nov 11 09:28:44, frame 5582 = Nov 13 23:58:08; 224964 s / 2082 frames = 108.05 s. Whole week: 604680 s / 5582 intervals = 108.33 s average because some images are missing. One day = 86400 / 108 = 800 frames = 26.67 s of video at 30 fps; playback is 3240x real time.
- **One frame = one day?**: no
- Where a program gets a frame's date: Two sources: (1) the label burned into every frame, to the second, in UTC (OCR is easy: white monospace text on black, fixed position); (2) for archived files, the YYYY-MM-DD in the file name (publish date = last data day + 1), then the formula. No separate date-only frame set, no per-frame image files.

## Gotchas

- Off-topic for an Earth challenge: this is the Sun. Bundle: 'Named in the official Jukebox challenge resource list: no'.
- Not one frame per day: 800 frames per day (108 s each). The 'daily' part is that the FILE is re-rendered once a day.
- Description conflict: top description says 'AIA-304', body text says the 171-angstrom filter and the thumbnail is named AIA_171. Files side with 171 (the gold colour is the usual 171 look; that last point is inference).
- Description conflict: the archive folders are described as holding videos 'published each day during the past week', but the listings hold 334 and 327 files going back to Sep 2025.
- Page size labels are stale: 60.0 MB vs 199,764,733 bytes actual; 15.1 MB vs 29,567,102 bytes actual.
- Missing source images make index-to-time drift (29 min at frame 1750 of the current week movie); use the burned label for exact time.
- Archive file-name date is the publish date; the data ends the previous UTC day. Archives have many missing days (88 of 422 and 85 of 412).
- Rolling URLs are overwritten every day around 06:30 UTC (01:30 Eastern): a demo that streams them live will change content between Saturday and Sunday. Use a dated archive URL or a local copy.
- Cross-origin pixel reading: HEAD with an Origin header on STWrolling_24hr_2048p30.mp4 returned 'access-control-allow-origin: https://tempo.multiverse.music'. A page on any other origin can play the video in a <video> tag but canvas getImageData will be blocked (tainted canvas); proxy it or serve a local copy.
- Brightness is processed ('color table and image processing applied'), so it is relative, not a physical unit; page text also warns of occasional dark circles (eclipses by Earth or Moon) that would read as sudden dimming.
- Added by the second agent: 5577 24 h file drifts too: T0+108k is 1 frame late at frame 250 and 1.6 frames late at frame 798. Archives miss about 21% of days, so a dated archive URL for a chosen day may 404. The archive 2026-11-14 file has the same byte size (29567102) and mtime as the rolling file.

Description: The Solar Dynamics Observatory, or SDO, orbits Earth in a geosynchronous path, providing a continuous view of the Sun. The instrument shown in this visualization observes ultraviolet light that is normally absorbed by Earth’s atmosphere, which is why these observations must be made from space.

These visualizations use the 171-angstrom filter of SDO’s Atmospheric Imaging Assembly, or AIA. You may also notice plasma eruptions, some of which are coronal mass ejections that can influence space weather. Bright flashes with X-shaped diffraction spikes are solar flares.
