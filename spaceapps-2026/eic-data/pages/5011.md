## Quick answer

- What it is: Yellow dots mark lightning detected by the LIS sensor on the ISS, Mar 2017 to Jul 2023, shown as a 10-day roving window; one movie is a rotating globe with a date dial, the other is a clean flat world map with no date on screen (NASA publishes its per-frame dates in labels.txt). Neither is one frame per day.
- Kind: data-animation
- Data source: Quality Controlled Lightning Imaging Sensor (LIS) on ISS Science Data V1 (NASA GHRC DAAC; point events with latitude, longitude and detection time). Background: Natural Earth 'Gray Earth' relief + MODIS MCD12C1 land cover; CPC cloud composite appears only in the opening close-up of the globe movie. (observation)
- Period covered: Flat SOS movie: clock runs 2017-03-01 01:25 to 2023-08-31 01:30 (labels.txt, 6001 lines, years from 1 Jan 2023 misprinted 2003), 2374.0 days; dots present until about the 22 Aug 2023 label, gone by the 30 Aug label. Globe movie: opens 'Mar 01 2017 03:27'; dial sweeps from early March 2017 to about late July/Aug 2023 (dot at t=213 s reads about late July by eye, +/-1 week). NASA text disagrees with itself (January vs March 2017); first label and first frame say 1 March 2017.
- Time step of the underlying data: Individual lightning events with a detection time (not gridded, not daily). Each frame draws every event inside a 10-day roving window (VERIFIED label '10 Day Roving Window' burned into the globe movie; NASA text: 'A roving window of 10 days').
- **One frame equals**: Globe movie (30 fps): phase 1 (t=0 to ~69 s, frames 0 to ~2070) = about 20 s of real time per frame, all inside 1 March 2017; phase 2 (t~72 to ~213 s) = 0.06 to 0.81 days per frame, mean 0.57 (analyst's table retained). Flat SOS movie (60 fps, ~6002 frames): NOT linear, eased. About 0 days per frame in the first second, 0.25 at t=11 s, 0.40 at t=16 s, 0.51 at t=23 s, peak 0.55 at t=50-56 s, 0.44 at t=76 s, 0.23 at t=91 s, 0.10 at t=98.5 s; mean 0.396. Each frame shows a 10-day window of events. Look the date up in labels.txt (line = frame number).
- **One frame = one day?**: no
- Where a program gets a frame's date: Flat SOS movie: labels.txt next to the movie (https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/vis/a000000/a005000/a005011/labels.txt), one 'DD Mon YYYY HH:MM' line per frame, no on-screen date; year 2003 from line 4927 means 2023. Globe movie: pixels only (phase 1 clock text; phase 2 year digits plus dial dot angle; use the analyst's lookup table). 4k and EIC cuts have the same pixels. No frame-set folders.
- Date label seen on screen: Globe movie (1920x1080 and 4k; same in the 7680x2160 EIC cut, far bottom-right). Exact text: t=0 'Mar 01 2017 03:27' (white, bottom-right, no time zone shown); t=54 'Mar 01 2017 12:42'; t=69 'Mar 01 2017 15:01' fading out; t=70-71 just '2017'; from t~74 a dial: bold '10 Day' / 'Roving Window' above a white ring, 'June' on top, 'March' left, 'Sept.' right, 'Dec.' bottom, bold year in the centre ('2017' ... '2023'), white dot on the ring; t=212 dial fading; t=213 to 216 only '2023', fading. Flat SOS movie and its print still: no text whatsoever.
- Update behaviour: Static, rendered once. All files have HTTP Last-Modified between Mon 25 Sep 2023 and Wed 18 Oct 2023 (movies 02 to 05 Oct 2023). No frame-set folders. The page record was touched 2025-03-28 but no media file changed.
- Status on 2026-11-14: static — newest data: SOS flat movie: clock ends 2023-08-31 01:30 (labels.txt); dots visible until about the 22 Aug 2023 label and none at the 30 Aug label, so the newest events are roughly mid-August 2023 (INFERRED). File-name suffix 07312023 is not the data end. Globe movie: dial about 15 Jul 2023 at t=212 s and about late July at t=213 s while fading (unresolved).
- View / camera: Two different pictures. (a) Globe movie: orthographic-looking 3D globe on a star field. Phase 1 (t=0 to ~69 s) is a zoomed, moving close-up with day/night shading, white clouds and pale diagonal swath strips (t=54 and t=60 show east Africa / Madagascar). Phase 2 (t~72 s on) is a full globe that ROTATES continuously: Africa/Atlantic centred at t=78, the Americas at t=102, eastern Pacific at t=108, Africa/Arabia at t=162, the Americas at t=210 and t=216. A given pixel is not a fixed place. (b) SOS movie: flat equirectangular 2:1 world map (4096x2048), fixed geography, lon -180..180 left to right, lat 90..-90 top to bottom; equator is a solid line at mid-height, dashed lines at about 23.6 N/S (y=236 and 404 of 640 in the preview).
- On-screen clutter: Globe movie: star field (white specks that a naive brightness threshold will count), clock or dial in the bottom-right corner, equator and tropic lines, country borders, clouds and swath strips in phase 1. No NASA insignia, no title, no colorbar, no units. Flat SOS movie: only the equator/tropic lines and faint country borders; a slightly lighter band from about 55 N to 54 S (y~125 to ~510 of 640) marks where the ISS sensor can see; nothing else.
- Clean variant: Yes: the flat SOS movie has no text, no dial, no stars, fixed geography, and its dates come from labels.txt (one line per frame). No alpha layers, no frame sets.
- What the colours mean: Not a colormap. Saturated yellow dots = lightning events detected by LIS inside the current 10-day window (presence only; a single colour, no intensity scale, no legend). Dark grey = land relief with a faint land-cover tint, darker grey = ocean. In phase 1 of the globe movie white = clouds (CPC composite).
- Fit as a sonification source: **good** — The flat SOS movie is about the easiest picture on the EIC list to read by program: fixed geography, one saturated colour on dark, no text, and an exact per-frame date in labels.txt. Weak points: dot counts are 10-day accumulations, the first ~6 s (window filling) and last ~1.5 s (window empty) carry almost no dots, speed is eased (0 to 0.55 days per frame), and ISS coverage is only about 55 N to 54 S.
- Suggested mapping: On the flat movie, for each frame find yellow pixels (R and G high, B low), count them in a grid of longitude columns x 3 latitude bands (north of 23.6 N, tropics, south of 23.6 S). Each cell that gains pixels triggers a short crackle: stereo pan = longitude, pitch = latitude band (north high, south low), loudness = number of new yellow pixels. Under it, a slow drone whose pitch follows the north/south balance, so a listener hears the lightning belt swing north in boreal summer and south in austral summer, roughly once per year of data.

## Gotchas

- Not one image per day. The dated sweep advances 0.17 to 0.81 days per frame, and the speed changes during the movie (slow start, about 4.7x faster in the middle, slow end).
- Every frame is a 10-day accumulation, so consecutive frames share almost all their dots. Count NEW yellow pixels (frame difference) if you want events rather than a smooth blob.
- The first ~69 s of the globe movie (about 32% of it: 2070 of 6501 frames) covers only about 11.5 hours of 1 March 2017 at 20.6 s per frame. Skip it for a multi-year piece.
- The clock in phase 1 shows no time zone. UTC is likely for ISS data but that is not on screen.
- The dial has no date text: year digits plus a dot angle. My 'March tick = 20 March' calibration is inferred from one year flip, so table dates can be up to about a week early.
- It is not known whether the dial marks the start, centre or end of the 10-day window.
- NASA's main description says data start January 2017; the first frame says 'Mar 01 2017' and the SOS description says March 2017.
- Coverage is only about 55 N to 54 S (ISS orbit); no dots at high latitudes means 'not observed', not 'no lightning'. Dots also reflect when the ISS passed overhead, not continuous monitoring.
- The globe movie has a star field: a plain brightness threshold will pick up stars. Threshold on yellow, not on brightness.
- The 7680x2160 EIC cut is HEVC (608 MB): not reliably playable in browsers. It is the same globe render centred in a wide frame.
- The second SOS file (..._debug_2023_sos_2048p60.mp4, 691.9 MB) is codec 'mpeg4' (MPEG-4 Part 2), not H.264: browsers will not play it. Its sampled frames look identical to the H.264 copy and carry no debug labels.
- No frame-set folders exist on this page; frames must be pulled from the movies.

## Open questions

- Why is dot density several times higher mid-movie (SOS t=49 s, t=74 s, t=93 s) than at t=7 s and t=99 s? Possibilities: the window is still filling / emptying at the ends, or the window is fixed in frames rather than days so faster playback shows more days. Until settled, do not treat yellow-pixel count as an absolute lightning rate.

- Globe rotation rate was not measured; from the sampled views it is roughly one turn per 85 to 90 s (rough inference).
