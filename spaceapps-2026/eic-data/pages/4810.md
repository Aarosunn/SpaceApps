## Quick answer

- What it is: Mostly stills and GIFs of OMI NO2 over the NE USA (March 2015-2019 vs March 2020) plus one 23 s movie stepping through March of each year 2015-2020; static since 2020.
- Kind: data-animation
- Data source: Aura OMI tropospheric NO2 column (datasets listed: Aura/OMI, Ozone, Blue Marble Next Generation basemap) (?)
- Period covered: March of 2015, 2016, 2018 and 2020 confirmed on screen; 2017 and 2019 inferred, not seen. Stills: March 2015-2019 average and March 2020. Also India NO2/SO2 and Florida/SE USA GIFs.
- Time step of the underlying data: Monthly mean per year (movie); the underlying OMI data are daily
- **One frame equals**: Not per-frame data. Each March holds about 4.27 s with cross-fades: 'March 2015' at t=0, 'March 2016' at t=4.27, 'March 2018' at t=12.80, 'March 2020' at t=21.33. Arithmetic: (2018-2015) = 3 years over 12.80 s = 4.27 s/year = about 128 frames at 30 fps. One frame is a slice of a month-year stage, not a day.
- **One frame = one day?**: no
- Where a program gets a frame's date: 2015-03
- Date label seen on screen: Yes: large rotated 'March 2015' etc., plus rotated 'Total NO2 Amount (kt): 0.496' (2015 and 2016 both read 0.496, 2018 = 0.528, 2020 = 0.347). Colorbar labeled NO2 in 10^15 molecules/cm2.
- Update behaviour: Static. mp4 Last-Modified Fri 10 Apr 2020 18:36 GMT, webm Sat 11 Apr 2020 20:14 GMT, stills 3-8 Apr 2020, GIFs Jun 2020. Page record updated 2025-01-19 without media change. No rolling window.
- Status on 2026-11-14: static — newest data: 2020-03 (March 2020)
- View / camera: Flat 2D regional map of the NE USA (Washington DC to Boston, Great Lakes to coast) with shaded-relief basemap and state borders; fixed camera, so one pixel-to-lat/lon calibration works for all years. A white tilted rectangle encloses the corridor used for the mass total.
- On-screen clutter: Rotated 'March YYYY' and 'Total NO2 Amount' text, white rectangle, borders, colorbar at bottom centre. Clean stills exist: avg2015-2019_NO2.jpg and 2020_NO2_final.jpg (1920x1080, no labels), no-label SE USA / Florida / India variants, standalone colorbar image cbar_NO2_0-5. No clean movie. Movie colorbar is 0-18 (ticks 0, 4.5, 9, 13.5, 18) but stills use 0-10 x10^15, so scales differ.
- What the colours mean: Light blue (low) through cream, orange, magenta, purple to near-black (high); unit 10^15 molecules/cm2 tropospheric NO2 column.
- Fit as a sonification source: **good** — A clean, fixed, flat before/after pair with one strong story (NO2 mass falls from 0.496 kt to 0.347 kt), but only a handful of monthly maps and no daily series, so it suits a compare button or one round, not a timeline.

##### Media group 320015 | widget: Basic text with HTML | title: (none)
Description: These recent improvements in air quality have come at a high cost, as communities grapple with widespread lockdowns and shelter-in-place orders as a result of the spread of COVID-19. One air pollutant, nitrogen dioxide (NO2), is primarily emitted from burning fossil fuels (diesel, gasoline, coal), coming out of our tailpipes when driving cars and smokestacks when generating electricity. Therefore, changes in NO2 levels can be used as an indicator of changes in human activity. However, care must be taken when processing and interpreting satellite NO2 data as the quantity observed by the satellite is not exactly the same as the NO2 abundance at ground level. NO2 levels are influenced by dynamical and chemical processes in the atmosphere. For instance, atmospheric NO2 levels can vary day-to-day due to changes in the weather, which influences both the lifetime of NO2 molecules as well as the dispersal of the molecules by the wind. It is also important to note that satellites that observe NO2 cannot see through clouds, so all data shown is for days with low amounts of cloudiness. If processed and interpreted carefully, NO2 levels observed from space serve as an effective proxy for NO2 levels at Earth's surface.
NASA's air quality  group is also monitoring other air pollutants, such as sulfur dioxide (SO2).  Major anthropogenic activities that emit SO2 include electricity generation, oil and gas extraction, and metal smelting. SO2 is emitted during electricity generation if the coal burned has sulfur impurities that are not removed (or not “scrubbed”) from the plant’s exhaust stacks.
For more information on what pollutants NASA satellites observe, visit the NASA Air Quality website.
