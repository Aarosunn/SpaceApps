## Quick answer

- What it is: A 5 min 17 s camera tour (rotating globe, then close-ups of the Kuroshio, Agulhas, Gulf Stream and Gulf of Mexico) of ECCO2-modelled ocean currents drawn as particle trails for 15 Jan 2021 to 25 Dec 2023; published once (static) as clean beauty, temperature and salinity versions plus compositing layers, all sharing one moving camera.
- Kind: data-animation
- Data source: ECCO2 High Resolution Ocean and Sea Ice Model (NASA/JPL, MIT, UT Austin; a model kept on track by satellite and in-situ observations). Its 3D velocity field moves virtual particles that are drawn with trails (3 model days long above 600 m depth, 6 days below). Trail color: beauty version = white above 600 m and temperature-colored at 600 m and deeper; temperature version = temperature; salinity version = salinity. Backdrop: Blue Marble Next Generation (MODIS) land imagery and ETOPO-30 bathymetry relief. (model)
- Period covered: Model time 2021-01-15 00:00 to 2023-12-25 00:00 = 1074 days (2.94 years), played in about 316.7 s. VERIFIED: the dates-only layer reads '15 Jan 2021' on movie frame 0 and '25 Dec 2023' on its last frame; NASA text says 'Dates range from 15 Jan 2021 to 25 Dec 2023' and 'covers the years 2021-2023'. The '600 meter and deeper' excerpt is frame indices 14000-16000 = 17 Mar 2023 08:50 to 8 Jul 2023 10:06 (113.05 days). Time zone is not stated.
- Time step of the underlying data: Unknown for the underlying ECCO2 output (not stated on pages 5425, 5479 or 5505). The rendering step is VERIFIED: 1074 days / 19000 frame intervals = 0.0565263 day = 81.40 minutes of model time per rendered frame index. INFERRED: frames are interpolated/advected between model outputs, because 81.4 min is not a round model cadence.
- **One frame equals**: Frame sets and 60 fps movies: one frame index = 1074 d / 19000 = 0.0565263 d = 1 h 21 min 24 s of model time. 30 fps movies show every second index: one movie frame = 0.1130526 d = 2 h 42 min 48 s. So one model day = 17.69 frame indices = 8.845 frames at 30 fps; 1 s of playback (any version) = 3.3916 model days; one month is about 8.98 s; one year about 107.6 s. Each drawn trail already spans 3 or 6 model days, so neighbouring frames are almost the same picture.
- **One frame = one day?**: no
- Where a program gets a frame's date: Compute it from the frame index with the formula (recommended). File names carry only the index, never a date. A separate dates-only layer exists (https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/vis/a000000/a005400/a005425/perpetual_ocean2_CLOSE_093_datesOnly_1080p30.mp4, 2,653,176 bytes; a 2160p60 MP4 of 10.6 MB; and the EXR frame set 'dates'): white 'DD Mon YYYY' at bottom-left on black, which would need OCR. No date is burned into any data movie on this page.
- Date label seen on screen: None on the beauty, temperature, salinity, 600 m, land, land-mask, sea-ice or equator files. Only the separate dates-only layer shows text, e.g. '15 Jan 2021' (white, bottom-left; 2-digit day, 3-letter month, year; no clock time, no time zone).
- Update behaviour: Static, rendered once. Frame files are dated 12-29 Nov 2024 and the 16:9 movies have Last-Modified 12 Nov to 16 Dec 2024. A hyperwall (5x3) beauty re-render was added later: frames dated 19-20 Mar and 22 and 24 Apr 2025, movie Last-Modified Wed, 23 Apr 2025 14:37:05 GMT. The newest file on the page is a SIGGRAPH logo PNG (Tue, 20 May 2025 17:40:37 GMT), the same day as the page record update. No rolling window and no scheduled re-render.
- Status on 2026-11-14: static — newest data: 2023-12-25 (model date on the last frame; 1055 days before 2026-11-14). The page is not a live feed, so this is not a lag.
- View / camera: 3D globe on black with a camera that moves almost all the time; not a flat map, so pixel (x,y) has no fixed latitude/longitude. Timeline measured on the land-mask layer (16:9 movie time, +/- 2 s; model dates from the formula): 0-72 s one full globe rotation (15 Jan - 15 Sep 2021); 72-88 s slow drift over the W Pacific; 88-106 s zoom to Japan; 106-120 s Kuroshio close-up, slow drift (9 Jan - 25 Feb 2022); 120-154 s pull out and cross the Indian Ocean; 154-166 s Agulhas close-up with a fully static camera (21 Jun - 31 Jul 2022; the only static stretch: 12 s = 360 frames = 40.7 model days); 166-200 s drift west; 200-212 s fast move across the Atlantic; 212-233 s approach; 233-262 s Gulf Stream close-up, slow drift (16 Mar - 22 Jun 2023; 4.4% of land-mask pixels change between 233.5 s and 261.5 s); 262-276 s move; about 277-281 s Gulf of Mexico, near static (Aug 2023); 282-317 s zoom out to a globe over the Americas (to 25 Dec 2023). Every version and layer on this page shares this camera path; the hw5x3 version is the same tour in a 2.96:1 frame (judged from 5 sampled frames and identical frame numbering). No fixed-camera variant exists on this page.
- On-screen clutter: Beauty 'clean_noDate' (16:9 and hw5x3): none - no date, title, colorbar, legend or logo. Temperature and salinity versions: a thin equator line with a small 'equator' label is burned in. All versions: thin political border lines on land, shaded 3D bathymetry on the sea floor, and a land texture that stays wintry (snow) whatever the date. Colorbars exist only as separate still images.
- Clean variant: Yes. The main beauty movie is already clean. Frame-aligned compositing layers are published separately (MP4 plus EXR set each): dates only; equator line and label only; sea ice only; land only with seasonally changing snow (LAND_ONLY2); land mask (white land on black, 48,502,803 bytes at 1080p30: https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/vis/a000000/a005400/a005425/perpetual_ocean2_CLOSE_097_LAND_MASK_1080p30.mp4 - handy for keeping only ocean pixels); and deep (600 m and below) currents for the Gulf Stream stretch only. There is no currents-only layer for the whole sequence, and the layers exist only for the 16:9 framing (none for hw5x3). EXR alpha channels were not inspected. A fixed-camera flat map of the same data is NOT on this page; it is on sibling page 5479 (see gotchas).
- What the colours mean: Beauty: trails that start above 600 m are plain white (no data value); trails at 600 m and deeper are colored by temperature, blue (-3 C) through light blue to white (22 C), ticks -3, 2, 7, 12, 17, 22. Temperature version: 0 C near-black navy, 5 indigo, 10 purple, 15 pink-salmon, 20 orange, 25 yellow (ticks every 5 C). Salinity version: 33 PSU teal, about 34.5 gray, about 35.3 orange, 36 red (ticks 33, 34, 35, 36). Arrow ends of the bars mean out-of-range values. Longer, denser, brighter trail bundles mean faster flow (a trail is 3 or 6 days of travel). Land colors are imagery, not data.
- Fit as a sonification source: **fair** — Strong points: very high-contrast flow shapes, no burned-in clutter, three frame-aligned variables (temperature, salinity, beauty), a land mask to isolate ocean pixels, and an exact frame-to-date formula. Weak points: the camera moves in all but 12 s of the 317 s, so most frame-to-frame pixel change is camera motion and zoom rather than ocean change, and a pixel has no fixed location; each region is seen in a different season and year; a frame is 2.7 h of model time with 3-6 day trails, so the data itself changes slowly; the full movies are 0.95-1.3 GB and SVS blocks cross-origin pixel reads. It works well as a narrated 'tour' soundscape and poorly as a per-pixel, per-day data sonification. The flat-map sibling render on page 5479 would rate good.
- Suggested mapping: Region-and-date tour. Use the camera timeline to announce the scene in speech or screen-reader text (rotating globe, Kuroshio, Agulhas, Gulf Stream, Gulf of Mexico) and the month (15 Jan 2021 + 3.39 days per second; a soft tick every 8.98 s = one month). Inside each scene, on the temperature version with land removed by the land-mask layer: mean temperature of the bright trail pixels (colorbar lookup) -> pitch (cold low, warm high); share of ocean pixels covered by bright trails (current strength, number of eddies) -> loudness and note density; left-right centroid of the warm pixels -> stereo pan. Optionally add salinity (same frame index) as timbre.

## Frame → time mapping

Linear, VERIFIED. datetime(n) = 2021-01-15T00:00 + n * (1074/19000) days = n * 4883.874 s, where n is the 5-digit number in a frame file name (0..19000; the hw5x3 set has even n only; the 600 m set has n = 14000..16000). Movies: (a) 16:9 1080p30 files, 9503 frames: n = 2*m - 4 for movie frame m >= 4 (the first four steps advance one index each), i.e. n = 60*t - 4; (b) hw5x3 810p30, 9531 frames: n = 2*m for m <= 9500, then the last picture is held for 1 s; (c) 2160p60, 19002 frames: n = k - 1 (checked on the start of the dates layer only). Rule of thumb: date = 15 Jan 2021 + 3.3916 days * t_seconds (error under 0.25 day). To pull one frame per model day from a 1080p30 movie take m_k = round(8.8454376*k + 2) for k = 0..1074 (day k = 15 Jan 2021 + k days). Checks made: 10 sampled labels and 20 day-flips in the dates-only movie (19 exact, 1 one frame late at an exact midnight boundary, n = 9500); 4 burned-in 'date HH:MM' + 'frame: N' labels on the sibling render 5479 (11800 -> 13 Nov 2022 00:15, 11818 -> 14 Nov 2022 00:40, 15400 -> 04 Jun 2023 12:07, 19000 -> 25 Dec 2023 00:00) all match to the minute; Gulf Stream eddies in the 5425 temperature movie at t = 256.74 s (n = 15400) match the 5479 frame labelled 04 Jun 2023.

## Gotchas

- NOT one frame per day: one model day = 8.845 frames of a 30 fps movie (17.69 frame indices). Neighbouring frames are 2.7 h (30 fps) or 1.36 h (index) apart and every trail already spans 3-6 days, so sonify at a coarser step (every 9th 30 fps frame is about 1 day).
- Moving camera in every file on this page. Only 154-166 s (Agulhas) is fully static; about 277-281 s (Gulf of Mexico) is near static; the Gulf Stream stretch 233-262 s drifts slowly; the published '600 m and deeper' excerpt drifts too and speeds up in its last ~4.8 s. Color statistics jump at zooms because of framing, not because the ocean changed.
- Time and place are confounded: each region is on screen for a different 1-3 month window (Kuroshio Jan-Feb 2022, Agulhas Jun-Nov 2022, Gulf Stream Mar-Jun 2023), so regions cannot be compared at the same date and no region shows a full seasonal cycle.
- A fixed-camera version exists only on sibling page 5479 'Ocean Currents in equirectangular projection' (listed under 'related' on 5425 but NOT in the EIC gallery list): https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/vis/a000000/a005400/a005479/perpetual_ocean2_EQUIREC_072_FINAL15_SOS_temperature_1024p30.mp4 - 2048x1024 flat map, H.264, 30 fps, 122.03 s, 140,113,363 bytes, Last-Modified Fri, 16 May 2025 20:28:58 GMT; burned-in 'DD Mon YYYY HH:MM' bottom-left and 'frame: N' bottom-right; n = 11800 + 60*t, so it covers only 13 Nov 2022 to 25 Dec 2023 (407 days), then holds the last frame. Beauty and salinity files with the same name pattern are listed by the API but were not probed.
- CORS: SVS answers every request with 'access-control-allow-origin: https://tempo.multiverse.music' (tested with Origin http://localhost:8000, Origin null and no Origin). A local page can play the MP4 in a plain <video>, but canvas getImageData and fetch on the remote file are blocked. Download the file and serve it from the same local origin. SVS does honour byte ranges (HTTP 206).
- Local server: this machine's Python 3.14.6 http.server has no Range support (its source never mentions 'Range'), so seeking inside a ~1 GB video served by 'python -m http.server' will not work properly; use a Range-capable static server or cut a short clip first. Pages opened from file:// usually cannot read video pixels either (general browser behaviour, not tested here).
- Sizes: full-length data movies are 947.7 MB to 1.28 GB at 1080p30 (24-33 Mb/s) and 3.6-5.4 GB at 2160p (90-136 Mb/s, too heavy for a browser); the only small data clip is the 600 m excerpt (120.9 MB). Every frame set is OpenEXR (17-33 MB per 4K frame; 507.5-525.4 GB per 4K data set, 707.2 GB for the hw5x3 set).
- Frame-count quirks (files win over the page): the page says 19002 items for beauty_all_depths but the listing has 19001 (0..19000). 2160p60 movies have 19002 frames (one extra at the start), 16:9 1080p30 movies have 9503 (start-up steps of one index), the hw5x3 movie has 9531 (final picture held 30 frames) against 9501 EXR frames. The offsets are at most 4 indices (5.4 h of model time).
- The land in the beauty, temperature and salinity movies is a fixed wintry texture (snow over N Asia at t = 62 s = Aug 2021; snow by the Great Lakes at t = 256.74 s = Jun 2023). Only the separate LAND_ONLY2 layer has seasonal snow that follows the date. Do not read the season from land pixels, and mask land (white snow) before treating 'white' as 'strong current' in the beauty version.
- Beauty colors are not one variable: white = any current above 600 m, blue-to-white = temperature of deep currents, and warm deep water is also near-white. Use the temperature version when a color must mean a number.
- Temperature and salinity movies carry a thin equator line and an 'equator' label; the beauty and temperature colorbar stills also contain a stray faint 'equator' word just under the bar.
- Naming traps: folder '9600x3240_16x9_30p' is not 16:9 (9600x3240 is the 5x3 hyperwall shape; its movie is 2400x810); page size labels are binary units ('903.8 MB' = 947,685,048 bytes); the 1080p30 land movie is named LAND_ONLY2 but the 2160p60 one LAND_ONLY.
- NASA's dates-layer sentence names only the beauty and temperature versions. The salinity version looks frame-aligned too (same eddies as the temperature movie at t = 110 s and t = 237 s) - INFERRED, not stated by NASA.

##### Media group 376523 | widget: Basic text with HTML | title: (none)
Description: This is a visualization of ocean currents around the world. NASA’s ocean model, Estimating the Circulation and Climate of the Ocean (ECCO)), is used to visualize the currents. The ECCO ocean circulation model incorporates observations from spacecraft, buoys, and other in situ measurements to keep the model accurate. ECCO is a joint project between NASA/JPL, MIT, and UT Austin. The model output used here is from ECCO-2 and covers the years 2021-2023.
In 2011, we used ECCO2 to create a visualization called Perpetual Ocean.  Perpetual Ocean continues to be extremely popular, but it only shows ocean currents on the surface.  In this new visualization, we use the ocean’s 3D velocity field to visualize some of the strongest ocean currents. We release virtual particles in the ocean and allow them to move with the ocean’s three dimensional velocity field. Each particle has a trail to better see its direction of movement. The particles initialized above 600 meters in depth have a trail length of 3 days, those initialized deeper than 600 meters have a trail length of 6 days. The particle trails help identify the strongest currents in the world that are squeezed in narrow belts on the western side of each ocean basin. These are called western boundary currents. The looping meanders in the boundary currents sometimes form turbulent rings (eddies) that can trap cold or warm waters in their centers and then separate from the main flow (see 2:30 of temperature version for an example for trapped warm water). In general, the western boundary currents are warm and salty.
The visualization starts from a global rotating view before slowing down to see the Western Boundary Current along the western edge of the Pacific Ocean along the coasts of Australia, and Asia. We zoom in to show the  Kuroshio Current off the coast of Japan. Along the Japanese coast, the current exhibits large meanders that can persist for many months in more or less the same location. The Kuroshio current has a temperature range of  20 to 25 °C. Its salinity can change seasonally with an average value of 34.5.
We then zoom out  and move over the Indian Ocean The Indian Ocean exhibits large variations in salinity. The western Indian Ocean is quite salty (>36) due to overflow inputs from marginal Seas (e.g. Persian Gulf and Red Sea). The East Indian Ocean is fresher (~35) due to river inputs from India.   The Indonesian Throughflow is quite fresh (33-34) and carries freshwater from the Pacific. We then zoom into the southern tip of Africa. The exchange of water from the Indian Ocean to the South Atlantic occurs here. The Agulhas Current is another Western Boundary current following the slope of the continental shelf closely. The continental shelf along the east coast of southern Africa is quite narrow and steep. This sloping topography stabilizes the Agulhas Current so that it shows no wide meanderings of the type familiar in other boundary currents such as the Kuroshio. The Agulhas Current overshoots the African continent, moving  into the South Atlantic. Then retroflects back to the Indian Ocean. At the retroflection, shedding of warm (20 to 25 °C ) and salty (~35.5)  rings happens. The eddies detached from the current have a lifetime of more than 2 years traveling across the south Atlantic ocean. These eddies are referred to as Agulhas Rings.
Another Western Boundary Current, called the Gulf Stream comes into view along the east coast of North America. The Gulf Stream forms at the Florida Straits. It’s one of the fastest currents on Earth with surface speed of up to 2.5 meters per second. In the Gulf stream, cold cores (mostly anticyclonic ones) form when the Gulf Stream meanders eastward leaving the coast of North America (off Cape Hatteras in North Carolina). The eddy diameters can be as large as 1000 km. Zooming into the Gulf Stream, we can see that the warm surface water (>25°C) moves poleward (white particle trails). The Gulf Stream is generally the warmest and saltiest western boundary current. There’s a return current underneath at depth below 500m (the blue particle trails) moving southward carrying cold waters from the pole. 
The loop currents in the Gulf of Mexico are very large eddies persisting in the Gulf. They bring the warm and highly saline Caribbean water into the Gulf. As we zoom out from the Gulf Stream, the salinity version shows that the Atlantic is generally much more salty than the Pacific.
Models like ECCO-2 help scientists to understand the characteristics of these currents better and ultimately understand how heat is transported globally in the Ocean.
